02. Energy Access & Equity

Why This Work Is Personal

My interest in resilient living began long before Banana Loop.

I grew up in India, in a hot and humid environment where the effects of heat, water shortages, and unreliable access to essential resources were part of everyday life. These were not abstract environmental challenges. They shaped how people studied, worked, managed their homes, and went about their lives.

I remember seeing a child faint during school assemblies held in the sun. I remember hearing about a young mother who died from summer exhaustion indoors, and a postman whose years of working through the summer heat ended in tragedy. Experiences like these left me with questions that have stayed with me: Why do ordinary environmental conditions become dangerous for some people? Why can a home, which should offer protection, become unsafe? And why does the ability to cope with heat depend so much on a person's circumstances?

I also experienced the everyday consequences of resource constraints. I remember studying for my twelfth-grade computer science examination by candlelight. Water availability and quality could not always be taken for granted, and making the most of what was available was simply part of life. These experiences shaped my understanding of resilience long before I had a word for it.

Over time, I began to see a connection between these everyday difficulties and the larger systems surrounding us. Heat is connected to buildings, electricity, and access to cooling. Water security is connected to energy, infrastructure, and how communities manage the resources available to them. Flooding is connected to how cities grow, how land is used, and what happens to rainwater once it reaches the ground.

These are not isolated problems. They are connected systems, and the consequences of their failure are not experienced equally.

My professional life took me into software engineering and data systems. For more than a decade, I have worked on building and improving complex technological systems. That experience gave me tools to approach difficult problems through engineering, data, and systems thinking. But it also made me increasingly interested in applying those skills to the systems that directly shape people's lives.

I want to understand how communities can maintain access to cooling during extreme heat, how distributed energy can improve resilience, how cities can manage water and recurrent flooding more intelligently, and how we can keep valuable materials in use instead of treating them as waste. I am interested not only in whether a solution works technically, but also in whether people can access it, afford it, and depend on it when they need it most.

For me, caring about the Earth is inseparable from caring about the conditions in which people live. The natural environment, the built environment, energy, water, and human well-being are deeply connected. Protecting one while ignoring the others will rarely be enough.

Banana Loop is my way of exploring these connections and working toward more resilient ways of living. I bring an engineering and technology background to these questions, along with personal experiences that remind me why the answers matter. I want to use that combination to investigate practical ideas, learn across disciplines, and contribute to systems that use resources more intelligently and make essential services more reliable and equitable.

This is not a departure from my engineering career. It is a direction in which I want to put that experience to work.

The idea behind the name

The banana plant is a remarkable example of a circular system. It provides food, leaves for plates, fibers for textiles, and new shoots for continued growth. Its organic matter eventually returns to the soil, enriching it for future growth. Banana Loop draws inspiration from this natural cycle of regeneration, resourcefulness, and renewal.
